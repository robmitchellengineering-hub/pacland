Plain English

What these boards mean, and how to use them

Every card above answers one question: has this claim been checked against something independent, and what happened? Not "is it a good model". Not "do we like it". Whether the evidence exists, and what it says.

What the grades mean

VERIFIEDEvery question we can ask has been asked and answered. The work reproduces. You can lean on it.
QUALIFIEDIt passes, but with a stated limit. Read the limitation before you act — it is the sentence that says where the evidence stops.
RESTRICTEDIt owes something. Part of what would make it trustworthy has not been shown.
REFUSEDSomething required fails. Not "we are unsure" — a specific check came back negative, and we can show you which one.

What the numbers mean

m is the number of independent things that were actually measured. The radius is how wrong the answer could still be with that many checks — it shrinks as sqrt(1/m), so four checks buy a wide error bar and twenty-four buy a narrow one. Extrapolation means the claim is about something that was never measured, and a confidence interval from a different region is not an error bar for it. Worth is the decision that follows: act, act with a hedge, or do not act.

How to use it

  • Before you act on a forecast — find the claim, not the model. If its row says do not act, the number you were given is not the number you need.
  • When choosing between two models — compare the verified column, not the accuracy. A model with a smaller error and no verified claims is a coin with a nicer face.
  • When someone sends you a result — ask which gates it passed and what would have falsified them. If that sentence does not exist, neither does the evidence.

Why you can trust the certificate itself

Because it is not written, it is generated. Every card is rebuilt from this repository's own evidence by a script, and python3 scripts/check_certificates_current.py fails if a committed certificate differs by a byte from a fresh run. Here is the manifest with a sha256 for each artifact — and here is what we refused: two of four parts fail the contract, and we publish those failures with the same prominence as the passes. A company willing to publish its refusals is the only kind worth hiring for analysis where being wrong is expensive.
